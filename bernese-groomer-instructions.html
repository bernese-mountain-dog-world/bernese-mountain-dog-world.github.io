Bernese Mountain Dog owner notes

What to Tell Your Groomer About a Bernese Mountain Dog: Feet, Feathers, Pants, Ears and Why Not to Shave

A plain-language brief you can hand over at drop-off, so your Berner comes home clean, comfortable and still looking like a Berner.

Anna Lindqvist · Bernese Mountain Dog World

Knowing what to tell your groomer about a Bernese Mountain Dog is one of the quiet ways you look after the dog who leans on your legs every evening. Many groomers mostly see doodles and poodles, and if you just say "a tidy," a Berner can come home with a bowl-cut look, short pants or a clipped belly. A clear brief protects the coat and makes the visit easier for everyone who loves that dog.

Quick answer: Ask for a bath, a full brush-out and blow-out of loose undercoat, a nail trim, and a scissor tidy of the feet so no hair sticks out past the pads. Ears are optional and should only be neatened. Ask the groomer to leave the feathers, pants and body coat at their natural length, and say clearly: no clippers on the body and no shave. The Bernese Mountain Dog Club of America puts it in capitals: "DO NOT shave your dog!"

Build your groomer instruction card

Pick what you want for each area. The card updates as you choose, and it flags any choice that goes against breed-club guidance. Copy it into a text or print it for drop-off.

The card follows the short list that experienced owners keep repeating: in a r/bernesemountaindogs thread called "What do you ask for at the groomers?", an owner who pays about $140 plus tip a month in a high-cost city learned from their breeder, an experienced event judge, that the ears, pants and feathers had been trimmed wrong. The feathers should have been longer, more of the pants kept, and the ears neatened evenly instead of looking like a bowl cut.

If seeing it helps more than reading it, this video from Bernese breeder and groomer Stacy Slade (SitStay) shows the tools she uses and how she neatens Bernese feet and ears step by step, which is exactly the "tidy, not a haircut" look this brief asks for.

What should a Bernese Mountain Dog groomer actually do?

Very little cutting, and a lot of brushing. The breed club's grooming guide says the dog is shown in natural coat, that "undue trimming is to be discouraged," and that apart from neatening the feet and ears, no trimming is needed.

That line comes from the BMDCA Info Series #13 on grooming (PDF), written by the national breed club for owners of pet and show dogs alike. It changes how you should think about a salon visit. You are not paying for a haircut. You are paying for the hard, hairy work most of us cannot do well at home: a thorough bath, a full drying, and getting the dead undercoat out down to the skin.

The services worth asking for

  • Bath and thorough rinse with a shampoo made for dogs.
  • Blow-out and brush-out of loose undercoat, reaching the skin, especially during a heavy shed.
  • Nail trim or grind, keeping nails short whether your dog is a companion or a show dog.
  • Feet tidy: hair between the pads trimmed so it no longer extends past them.
  • Optional ear neatening, only if you prefer it.

The coat you are protecting

The Royal Kennel Club breed standard describes the Bernese coat as "soft, silky with bright natural sheen, long, slightly wavy but should not curl when mature." Every instruction on your card is really about keeping that long, natural, slightly wavy coat intact, so the dog you hug after the appointment still feels like your dog.

How should a groomer trim Bernese feet?

Trim the hair between the pads so none extends beyond them, then neaten the outline of the foot with scissors. The BMDCA says this improves traction on slippery floors, prevents ice balls between the toes in winter, and means less dirt carried into the house.

Owners often call the untrimmed version "Grinch feet," because the long tufts curl out between the toes. On hardwood or tile, those tufts can make a big dog slide when they get up, which matters for a 100-pound family member who wants to follow you from room to room.

Clippers or scissors?

The breed club guide mentions both electric clippers and straight scissors for the pads. For the top and outline of the foot, ask for scissors, so the result is a neat, natural-looking foot rather than a bare poodle foot. If your groomer prefers clippers between the pads, that is fine; just make sure they stay on the underside.

A note for snowy climates

Not every owner wants short feet. In the "Tips about grooming? (11month old)" thread, one owner in Sweden prefers to keep the foot hair and simply check for icy buildup after walks, while the breed club recommends trimming to prevent ice balls. Both are choices made out of care. Pick the one that fits your winters, and tell your groomer which one you want.

Should the groomer cut the feathers and pants?

No. In breed-club and breeder guidance, the leg feathering and the long "pants" on the back of the thighs are left at their natural length. At most, ask for straggly ends to be tidied.

This is the instruction that goes missing most often, because "tidy up" means something different to a groomer who spends all day shaping doodles. In the 31-comment thread "Is this a bad grooming?", experienced owners kept repeating the same list: the only things that usually need trimming on a Berner are the feet, the fur between the pads, possibly the sanitary area, and possibly the ears. The pants and the front feathers are usually left alone.

When a light trim makes sense

Life with a Berner is muddy, and some owners make practical exceptions. In the "What do you ask for at the groomers?" thread, one owner trims the feathers on the back legs because they collect dried leaves in fall and snow chunks in winter. That is a reasonable household choice for a pet that is not shown. The key is that you decide, rather than a groomer deciding for you.

The sanitary area

A small scissor tidy under the tail keeps things clean for many owners. It should be a light trim, not a clipped-short back end. If your dog came home with the pants clipped away, it was probably done with clippers rather than scissors, which is worth mentioning politely next time.

What about the ears?

Ear trimming is a personal preference. Some owners treasure the soft "ear fuzzies," while others like a neater ear where the shape shows. Either way, ask for thinning shears and an even length, not a blunt cut.

The BMDCA guide describes using thinning shears or hand stripping to neaten the ear so that its shape is apparent, and notes that double-sided thinning shears give a smoother, finished look. That is the opposite of the "bowl cut" the owner in the first Reddit thread ended up with. If you are unsure, start with nothing. As the breed club reminds owners, it is better to cut too little than too much, and it will always grow back.

Why shouldn't you shave a Bernese Mountain Dog?

Because the breed club says not to, plainly and in capital letters, and because the breed's natural long coat is part of what the standard describes. A shave or body clip removes the coat the breed is meant to have, and it takes a long time to return.

Owners in the "Is this a bad grooming?" thread share why they avoid it: the most upvoted reply simply says they have never cut their Berner's hair, only bathed and brushed. Others report that guard hairs on double-coated dogs can grow back unevenly after being cut very short. Treat those as owner experiences rather than lab results, but they line up with the BMDCA's instruction.

"But it's summer, and they look so hot"

We understand the impulse; it comes from love. The better summer plan is a thorough de-shed and blow-out so loose undercoat is not trapped in the coat, plus the everyday cool-weather walking habits you already use. If you are tempted to ask for a summer shave, ask instead for a de-shedding bath and a longer brush-out appointment.

How do you prepare your Berner for the groomer?

Start early and keep it gentle. Groomers in these owner threads recommend introducing the salon in puppyhood, and the breed club stresses slow introductions to the table, the dryer and tools, with frequent breaks and plenty of praise and treats.

In the "First Attempt At Groomer Failed" thread, a professional groomer of 15 years said her biggest concern was that a 9-month-old Berner was only then having a first full grooming experience, and that being fine for a nail trim is not the same as tolerating a whole visit. Another groomer in the same thread books first appointments at 12 weeks and uses the first two or three visits mainly to introduce tools, sounds and smells. In the 11-month-old thread, a couple who own a grooming salon and live with Berners said the dog should have started months earlier just for baths and desensitization, and that now is the next best time.

If your dog is already a big adolescent, do not panic. Our guide to the Bernese teenage phase explains why handling skills can wobble at this age and how to rebuild them in small, easy steps before a full appointment.

The dryer deserves its own plan

The BMDCA notes that many dogs are very afraid of the noise and feeling of a high-velocity dryer at first. Its advice is to start slowly on low speed, reduce airflow or increase distance, never force the situation, and use lots of praise and treats. Put this on your card if your dog is nervous, so the groomer knows before the dryer comes on.

Getting there calmly

A relaxed arrival makes the whole appointment easier. If your Berner rides in a crate, our crate and SUV fit checklist helps you set the car up so the trip to the salon is not the stressful part. And if your dog digs in their heels at the salon door, the patient, reward-based steps in our article on what to do when a Bernese refuses to walk work just as well for a hesitant entrance.

Drop-off checklist: what to say in the first two minutes

  1. Hand over your card from the tool above, or show it on your phone.
  2. Say the breed out loud: "This is a Bernese; the breed is kept in natural coat."
  3. State the no-go first: "No clippers on the body, and please don't shave any area except between the pads."
  4. Point to each area: feet, ears, feathers, pants. Touching them while you talk avoids misunderstandings.
  5. Mention handling notes: first full visit, dryer nerves, or a dog who needs to lie down for breaks.
  6. Ask one question back: "How do you usually finish a Bernese?" The answer tells you whether they know the breed.
  7. Agree on a call if they find heavy mats, so you decide together instead of coming home to a surprise clip.

Common mistakes that lead to a bad Berner groom

  • Saying only "a tidy." Without specifics, many groomers default to the shaping they do most often.
  • Booking a short slot. A full de-shed and drying on a big double-coated dog takes time; rushing invites clippers.
  • Skipping home brushing between visits. The BMDCA recommends weekly brushing, which keeps mats from forming and keeps more hair in the brush than around the home.
  • Bathing too often. The breed club notes that excessive bathing can cause as many problems as too little; many owners book every few months and brush weekly in between.
  • Waiting until the dog is huge before the first full visit, as groomers in the owner threads warn.
  • Not saying anything after a bad trim. Calm, specific feedback ("please leave the pants natural next time") usually fixes it, and the coat will grow back.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a Bernese Mountain Dog see a groomer?

There is no single rule. The breed club recommends weekly brushing at home and says bathing frequency depends on how dirty your dog gets, warning against excessive bathing. Owners in Bernese forums describe anything from monthly baths to a visit every three to four months with weekly home brushing.

Should a groomer trim between a Bernese's paw pads?

Most owners ask for it. The BMDCA describes trimming the hair between the pads so none extends beyond them, for better traction, fewer ice balls in winter and less dirt carried inside. Some owners in very snowy places prefer to keep more foot hair and check for ice instead.

Is it okay to shave a Bernese Mountain Dog in summer?

The Bernese Mountain Dog Club of America says plainly: do not shave your dog. Ask instead for a de-shedding bath and a thorough blow-out of loose undercoat, and keep up weekly brushing at home.

Do Bernese ears need trimming?

No, it is a personal preference. If you want neater ears, ask for thinning shears and an even length so the ear shape shows, rather than a blunt cut.

What if my groomer cut the pants and feathers short?

Give calm, specific feedback for next time and use a written instruction card. The breed club's own guidance is reassuring here: it is better to cut too little than too much, and the hair will grow back.

When should a Bernese puppy first visit the groomer?

Groomers in owner communities recommend starting in puppyhood; one experienced groomer books first visits at 12 weeks and uses the first two or three appointments just to introduce tools and sounds. Starting later is still possible with slower, shorter visits.

Speak up for the coat they were born with

Telling your groomer exactly what you want for your Bernese Mountain Dog is not being picky; it is being your dog's voice in a room where they cannot speak for themselves. Ask for a clean, brushed-out coat, tidy feet, nails done, ears only if you like, and feathers, pants and body left natural. Your Berner will come home comfortable, still looking unmistakably like the dog you love.
