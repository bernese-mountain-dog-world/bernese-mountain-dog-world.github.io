How to Get a Bernese Mountain Dog to Ride Calmly and Lie Down in the Car

A fair training plan for settling, ramp access, and getting a 100-lb dog back onto the ground.

Wondering how to get a Bernese Mountain Dog to ride calmly and lie down in the car when your passenger fills the entire back seat? You can start again without blaming yourself for earlier noisy rides: separate getting aboard, settling, and traveling into lessons your dog can actually finish. For a 100-lb dog, plan the exit before the journey as carefully as the entrance.

Quick answer: Teach a comfortable down on a mat at home, practice it in a parked car, and introduce the engine and movement separately. Use a properly installed travel restraint throughout journeys. Teach a ramp on the ground before using it at the vehicle, and wait for a release cue before coming out.

Why won't my Bernese lie down in the car?

First check whether lying down is physically possible in the space you have provided, then identify which part of the journey changes the behavior. A dog who settles while parked needs a different lesson from one who cannot enter willingly.

Write an observation instead of a label

Replace “he is stubborn” with something you can reproduce: “he lies down until I close the hatch,” or “he stands when we turn onto the main road.” Take notes while parked, or ask a passenger to observe during travel. The driver should concentrate on driving.

Dogs Trust's travel guidance explains that passing sights, engine sounds, and vehicle movement can be unfamiliar or frustrating. It recommends gradual introductions, gentle short journeys, and avoiding forced travel when a dog is frightened. That supports a smaller next lesson, rather than a louder down command.

Use an observation log with columns for loading, door closing, engine starting, movement, and unloading. Record the first point where an easy response disappears. Do not turn ordinary training into a diagnosis; if your dog seems unwell or the change is sudden, pause and ask your veterinarian for individual advice.

How do you teach a relaxed down before driving?

Build the behavior at home before asking for it in the vehicle. Reward lying on a portable mat, add a release word, and practice quiet pauses without constantly prompting your dog.

Make the mat a familiar destination

Choose a mat your Berner can actually stretch onto, then place it somewhere uneventful. Reward looking at or stepping onto it. If your dog already understands “down,” ask once when the dog is on the mat and reward there. Otherwise, work on the down separately with a reward-based trainer before adding the car.

Trainer Pat Miller's mat-training instructions describe reinforcing mat-related behavior, rewarding downs, adding a release cue, and increasing duration in small increments. Repeatedly getting up is a reason to make the duration easier. You do not need to hold your dog in position to prove the cue works.

Reward the position you want to keep

As a suggested practice arrangement, keep treats beside you rather than in front of the dog's nose. Deliver the reward low on the mat so your hand does not invite a jump toward your face. Then pause. If every reward starts another busy training performance, make the setting quieter and ask for less.

Use a simple release word such as “all done” before inviting the dog off. Practice with household distractions separately from duration. Changing the room, adding a visitor, and extending the wait in the same attempt makes it hard to tell what your dog has learned.

Where should a large Berner lie during the ride?

Choose an installed travel arrangement that fits both the dog and the vehicle. Check the actual lying space with your dog while parked; a cargo-area measurement alone does not show whether the dog can settle comfortably.

Check the whole position

Look at the paws, elbows, chest, and head when the dog lies naturally. Check whether the mat slides or bunches and whether the door can close normally. Follow the restraint manufacturer's fitting instructions rather than shortening a connector until the dog has no option but to stay down.

Our Bernese crate and SUV fit checklist helps organize those measurements. Leave the completed setup in place for parked practice so your dog learns the arrangement that will actually be used. A loose cushion or familiar blanket can be part of the environment, but it does not replace the travel restraint.

How do you get a 100-lb dog into and out of the car?

For a dog you cannot comfortably handle, plan an access route the dog can walk. A suitable ramp can turn loading into a learned sequence; repeatedly improvising a lift should not be the routine you depend on.

Practice the surface before the height

The Premier Pet ramp manual's training section starts with the ramp flat on the ground and adds height gradually. Its operating instructions also call for a stable surface, inspection, and keeping within the specified load capacity. Apply your own ramp's instructions, since attachment methods and permitted setups differ.

Here is a suggested rehearsal: place the ramp flat as directed, let your dog investigate, reward a willing step, and build toward crossing the full surface. Stand alongside rather than pulling from the far end. If your dog steps off, reset without turning the leash into a tow rope. Practice returning across it before moving the lesson to the car.

Arrange the approach and landing

At the vehicle, park where there is room for the entire approach and for you to walk beside your dog. Clear bags from the landing area. Set the ramp up before inviting your dog forward, and check its placement each time. Keep other household members out of that narrow working area.

A rear hatch and a side door present different approaches. Do not assume a ramp fits because it reaches the opening: check its intended contact points and the space for the dog to step off. If the available route cannot accommodate your dog and equipment, change the loading location or vehicle arrangement.

If you cannot load without lifting, ask for an individual handling demonstration from a qualified professional who can see the dog, vehicle, and your capabilities. This guide does not prescribe a solo lifting maneuver for a 100-lb animal.

What is the step-by-step plan for calm car rides?

Use the sequence below as a practice menu, not a calendar. Advance when the current task is repeatably easy; returning to an earlier step is useful information, not a failure.

  1. Prepare before bringing the dog out. Install the travel setup, clear the entrance, and have the access equipment ready. Pick a time without a departure deadline.
  2. Rehearse approaching and leaving. Walk toward the parked car, reward an easy approach, and walk away again. Entry is not required on every approach.
  3. Practice boarding and unloading. Use the familiar ramp or access route. Finish with a controlled return to the ground while the rest of the exercise is still easy.
  4. Bring the mat into the parked car. With the engine off, invite the familiar down. Reward in position, release, and finish without taking a journey.
  5. Add door movement. Start with the amount of movement your dog already accepts. Practice closing separately from starting the engine, while keeping the dog supervised.
  6. Add the engine outdoors. Park in an open, ventilated location. A helper can observe the dog while the driver handles the controls. End before the dog needs repeated prompts.
  7. Try an easy journey. Choose a quiet route with a convenient return and smooth driving. Let a passenger observe rather than asking the driver to reach back or feed treats.
  8. Rehearse arrival. Park, secure the leash before release, deploy the access equipment, and invite a controlled exit. The destination is part of the lesson.

Change one feature between attempts

For example, if your dog settles with the engine off but gets up when it starts, keep the vehicle stationary for the next practice. Do not add movement just because the dog eventually lies down once. If the first journey works, repeat a similar easy outing before introducing a busy destination.

For the walk before or after practice, use a familiar routine rather than an extra demanding outing intended to exhaust your passenger. Our Bernese daily walking routines can help you plan the surrounding day. Keep a short log of what worked so another family member can reproduce the lesson.

How do you prevent a rush out at arrival?

Teach waiting at the opening separately from coming down the ramp. Arrange the leash and access route before giving the exit cue, rather than trying to organize them around an already moving dog.

Rehearse when the destination is boring

Practice in your usual parked location first. Open the door only as far as you can manage while retaining control through the installed setup. Reward a pause, close gently, and repeat an easier version if your dog pushes forward. Keep hands, paws, and the leash clear of moving door edges.

Once the pause is familiar, add the exit cue when everything is ready. Have a helper manage nearby children or bags so the handler can concentrate on the dog. After unloading, move away from the vehicle before rearranging equipment.

If your dog tries to squeeze against your legs during loading, teach a waiting spot beside the approach. The same idea of providing a clear alternative appears in our guide to Berner leaning and walking between legs. Affection is welcome; a crowded ramp entrance simply needs another place for it.

Common mistakes that make practice harder

Look for a task you can simplify before deciding your dog needs firmer handling. A more manageable setup gives both of you a fresh start.

  • Repeating “down” throughout the drive. Practice the response while parked. During travel, the driver needs attention available for the road.
  • Using a treat to pull the dog beyond its confidence. Reward willing progress and leave room to stop the lesson. Reaching the seat is not the only useful result.
  • Starting with the ramp at full height. Return to the ground-level lesson if the surface itself remains unfamiliar.
  • Making every car session end at the exciting park. Include parked practice and ordinary returns home so boarding does not always announce an immediate party.
  • Rewarding only after the dog gets up. Plan a reward while the desired down is still happening, then use the release cue deliberately.
  • Comparing your dog with an edited training clip. Record your own earliest easy step. The useful comparison is with your previous attempt.

For a young dog, keep the same cues across the household. Our Bernese teenage-phase guide offers context for maintaining clear routines as your dog grows. If attempts remain overwhelming, get a reward-based trainer to observe rather than repeatedly rehearsing a difficult journey.

Frequently asked questions

Should I make my Bernese lie down for the entire journey?

Teach a comfortable down, but do not use restraint length or physical pressure to force it. Follow the travel equipment instructions and practice the response while parked before asking for it during a journey.

Can I teach this without lifting my 100-lb dog?

Plan a suitable walk-on access route, such as a ramp fitted according to its instructions. Begin training with the ramp on the ground. If you cannot arrange loading without lifting, get individual handling advice before continuing.

What if my dog lies down only when the car is parked?

Keep the parked-car behavior and introduce engine sound separately. Once that is easy, try a short, gentle journey. Ask a passenger to observe; the driver should not turn around to manage the lesson.

How long should it take to get calm car rides?

This plan sets no deadline. Use willing boarding, comfortable settling, and a controlled exit as your progress checks. If a stage remains overwhelming, return to an easier task and ask a reward-based trainer to observe.

Should I keep driving until my dog gives up barking?

Do not use a long journey as the training exercise. Practice below the point where the attempt becomes overwhelming, and build gradually. If your dog cannot cope with necessary travel, seek individual professional help with the plan.

A calmer ride starts before you turn the key

Getting a Bernese Mountain Dog to ride calmly and lie down in the car begins with an achievable next lesson: an easy approach, a familiar mat, or a controlled ramp crossing. Keep the setup consistent, practice the exit, and give yourself permission to restart at the stage your dog understands. You can improve tomorrow's routine without punishing yesterday's mistakes.
