Bernese Mountain Dog Teenage Phase: What Regresses and How to Rebuild It

A realistic timeline and practical resets for coming indoors, walking, and household manners.

Anna Lindqvist · Bernese Mountain Dog World

Living through the Bernese Mountain Dog teenage phase can feel like sharing a home with a familiar puppy whose listening skills have suddenly become optional. Coming indoors, walking past another dog, and leaving the laundry alone may all need a simpler lesson again. The useful question is which situation needs rebuilding today, rather than which birthday will fix everything.

Quick answer: Prepare for changing behavior during the latter part of puppyhood and keep supporting skills into young adulthood. A Berner's adult appearance does not guarantee adult consistency, and there is no proven breed-wide settling deadline. Reduce distractions, reward the response you want, and judge progress by repeatable everyday behavior.

When does the Bernese teenage phase start?

There is no validated starting month for every Berner. Breed guidance describes eight- to twelve-month-old Berners as still immature, while general dog research shows that responsiveness can change earlier in the first year.

The Bernese Mountain Dog Charitable Foundation's puppy guidance cautions against expecting a yearling to behave like a fully mature dog. It also describes young Berners as less behaviorally collected than mature three- or four-year-olds. That is experienced breed guidance, not a controlled study establishing the duration of adolescence.

Use age as a planning prompt

Set up your household for a large learner before the old puppy routine stops working. Keep rewards near the door, put laundry behind a closed door, and agree on the same coming-indoors cue. These preparations remain useful whether your dog becomes more distractible early, late, or barely at all.

Do not wait for a dramatic personality change before practicing. If your puppy currently turns toward you easily, reward that response in several ordinary places. Preserve a quiet practice location that you can return to when a new environment proves too demanding.

Why owners give different settling ages

One household may mean sleeping quietly after dinner; another means walking through a busy park without pulling. Those are different outcomes. Ask what a reported improvement actually looked like before comparing your dog with someone else's. A calm living room evening and a reliable outdoor response deserve separate entries in your notes.

For your own dog, define settling as an observable routine: resting while you eat, returning from the yard, or walking past a familiar driveway with slack in the leash. Pick one outcome to work on first. Avoid making a vague impression of being “good now” your only measure.

Has my dog forgotten the training?

A weaker response in a harder situation does not demonstrate that learning has vanished. Compare the same cue in a quiet place and the difficult location before deciding what needs reteaching.

In Asher and colleagues' 2020 canine adolescence study, dogs were less responsive to their carer's familiar sit cue at eight months than at five months, while responding to a stranger improved. The dogs came from a guide-dog population, not a Bernese sample. This supports the possibility of a temporary, context-dependent change; it cannot supply a Berner-specific finishing age.

Run a comparison instead of an argument

Try the familiar response in your usual quiet room. Then try it near the open back door without expecting a full yard return. If the first version works and the second does not, start teaching at the doorway. Repeating a louder cue from across the lawn adds pressure without clarifying the missing step.

Write down the setup as carefully as the result. “Turned toward me in the kitchen” and “continued sniffing beside the fence” are useful observations. “Knows it but refuses” combines an observation with an explanation you have not tested.

How do you rebuild coming when called?

Practice close by in an enclosed, quiet place before asking your Berner to leave something exciting. Reward arrival, then sometimes release the dog back to an allowed activity so coming to you does not always end the outing.

Make the next repetition achievable

  1. Prepare the reward before starting, keeping it out of sight rather than waving it as the cue.
  2. Begin when your dog is available to listen, not already absorbed in the hardest distraction.
  3. Give the familiar cue once. Mark the turn and reward beside you when the dog arrives.
  4. Pause together briefly, then release to an allowed sniff or activity.
  5. Move farther from the distraction if the turn becomes difficult. Shorten the distance to you before adding challenge again.

These are adjustable practice steps, not a promise of success within a fixed number of days. The video above demonstrates the underlying setup. Begin with its foundations rather than copying the most difficult demonstration straight away.

Separate practice from necessary transitions

If you must leave now, do not make that moment your hardest training test. Arrange an ordinary managed transition and practice the missing response later. Keep your cheerful training cue from becoming a repeated soundtrack to an argument at the door.

Why are leash manners and yard returns slipping?

Check what the dog gains from moving away and what happens after returning. Build an easier version that makes checking in worthwhile before asking for the full walk or immediate trip indoors.

Rebuild walking in small pieces

Choose a quiet stretch and reward a voluntary glance or a few willing steps near you. Pause to sniff by invitation. Add the interesting driveway or passing activity only after the easier section feels manageable. If stopping and lying down is the specific problem, use the blog's plan for rebuilding willing walking rather than treating every interruption as pulling.

Separate these goals in your notes: walking with a loose leash, responding to a turn, and moving away from another dog. A successful practice of one does not automatically demonstrate the others. Choose a route that lets you rehearse the skill you actually intend to teach.

Make coming indoors a separate lesson

Start near the threshold with an open route into the room. Reward crossing inside, then occasionally return outdoors together when circumstances allow. Practice before your usual departure deadline. Do not spend the whole yard visit ignoring the dog and then ask for the most difficult response only when the interesting time ends.

For outings, distinguish transport setup from training expectations. The SUV crate fit checklist can help organize the physical arrangement, but a suitable arrangement does not teach waiting, entering, or leaving on cue. Rehearse those transitions separately in a quiet setting.

What should you do when chewing and mouthing return?

Manage access to household objects and prepare an appropriate activity before the familiar difficult moment. Reward the alternative you want instead of making possession of a sock the beginning of an exciting chase.

Dogs Trust's behavior guidance recommends suitable toys, predictable downtime, and planning activities before mouthing begins. It also advises against punishment that can add frustration. These are management principles, not evidence that every adolescent Berner will mouth or chew.

Change the evening setup

Put tempting household items away before sitting down. Have a familiar toy available and reward choosing it. If lively play keeps spilling into grabbing clothes, finish with a quieter, familiar activity rather than repeatedly restarting the same exciting game. Create a manageable separate space when you cannot supervise.

Keep the record specific: what was available, what happened immediately beforehand, and what alternative worked. Do not count every incident as a new failure of obedience. If the pattern is escalating or you cannot manage the interaction, ask a qualified reward-based trainer for individual help.

A practical reset plan you can repeat

Use a short review cycle focused on one skill. The steps below are an editorial practice framework, not a tested treatment protocol or a timetable for maturity.

  1. Choose the outcome. Write “come across the threshold when called” instead of “listen better.” Everyone in the household should know what counts as completion.
  2. Find the easy version. Reduce distance, distractions, or duration. If necessary, return to the room where you originally taught the response.
  3. Arrange the environment. Prepare rewards, close unneeded doors, and remove objects that would compete with the lesson. Avoid starting while rushing to leave.
  4. Practice briefly. Stop while the setup is still working. Finish with an ordinary enjoyable activity rather than adding a surprise test.
  5. Record the response. Note independent successes and extra help separately. A response after showing food is useful information, but it is different from responding to the cue.
  6. Change one feature. Try a slightly more interesting location while keeping distance easy. Return to the previous setup after difficulty.
  7. Review the pattern. Compare like with like. Improvement in the kitchen should not be judged against yesterday's hardest park encounter.

Keep a tiny training log

Use the columns “place,” “cue,” “what happened,” and “next adjustment.” You do not need a score for your whole dog. A note that the yard return worked before play but not afterward already suggests a more useful next session than labeling the entire day a disaster.

Common mistakes that keep the routine difficult

Avoid changing every variable, repeating cues continuously, or waiting for adulthood to do the teaching. Make the lesson smaller when the current version is not working.

  • Expecting size to equal maturity. Keep support in place even when the dog looks grown.
  • Practicing only at maximum difficulty. A busy route can be an outing without being the right classroom.
  • Ending all fun after coming back. Include ordinary practice returns that lead to another allowed activity.
  • Chasing household objects. Organize access and rehearse exchanges during calm play.
  • Adding more demands after success. Finish a manageable session instead of raising the bar until the dog cannot respond.
  • Comparing unlike situations. Track your dog's own repeatable routine rather than someone else's birthday milestone.

If a walking reset remains difficult, revisit the easier route and willing-start exercises before adding another demand.

Frequently asked questions

When will my Bernese Mountain Dog finally settle?

There is no guaranteed age. Use breed guidance as context, then track specific routines such as resting through dinner or returning indoors. Maturity and a practiced response are different things; keep teaching the routine you want.

Can a one-year-old Berner still act like a puppy?

Yes. The breed guidance discussed above cautions against adult expectations for yearlings. Keep instructions clear and practice manageable rather than treating adult appearance as proof that support is unnecessary.

Should I stop rewarding skills my dog already knows?

Keep rewarding useful responses, especially while rebuilding them in distracting places. Prepare rewards before practice and gradually vary the context. Avoid turning a difficult repetition into a negotiation with food held in front of the dog.

What if coming when called works indoors but not in the yard?

Practice at the threshold and close to you before attempting the whole yard. Reward arrival and sometimes resume an allowed outdoor activity. The contrast tells you where to begin teaching, rather than proving the dog has forgotten everything.

Does more exercise solve teenage behavior?

Do not use exhaustion as the training plan. Arrange manageable outings, quiet downtime, and focused practice of the missing response. Running around and learning to return indoors are separate activities.

When should I get training help?

Ask a qualified reward-based trainer when the pattern is escalating, you cannot manage daily transitions, or easier setups still leave you stuck. Bring notes about the location, trigger, response, and strategies already tried.

Keep the goal smaller than the whole teenage phase

The Bernese Mountain Dog teenage phase is easier to navigate when you measure one routine at a time. Rebuild the achievable version, prepare the environment, and record what changes. Your next useful milestone is a willing return or a calmer transition, not an unproven birthday deadline.
