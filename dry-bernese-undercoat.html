How to Dry a Bernese Mountain Dog After Rain, Swimming or a Bath

Home›Guides›Drying a wet Berner

To dry a Bernese Mountain Dog well, you have to finish the layer you cannot see: the undercoat against the skin, not just the shiny black hair on top. A Berner can look dry across the back while the pants, the belly and the fold behind each ear are still cold and wet, and that hidden damp is what this routine goes after.

Quick answer: Rinse off lake or pool water, squeeze and press with towels (no scrubbing), then move air through the coat section by section, parting the hair until you reach skin that feels dry. Use cool or gently warm air, keep a loud dryer away from the head, and check ears and the damp spots again later in the day.

    Skin check: tick a zone when it is dry down to the skin

    Think of drying as recovery time for both of you. A swim or a wet hike spends energy, and a calm, repeatable rhythm gives some back: your dog gets to rest on something absorbent, and you stop wrestling a soggy giant around the kitchen. The goal is a pace you can keep up all season, not a marathon after every shower.

    This short demonstration from SitStay with Stacy Slade shows a forced air dryer used on a Bernese after a bath, and how a puppy can be taught to put up with it.

    Why does a Bernese undercoat stay wet so long?

    The dense undercoat holds water close to the skin while the longer outer coat covers it, so the surface dries first and misleads your hand. Mats make it worse because they trap moisture in place.

    Cornell University's canine health center puts the risk plainly: a dog who swims, wades or is just out in a warm summer rain is prone to hot spots if a thick coat does not dry fully. A hot spot is a patch of inflamed, often infected skin that a dog keeps licking or scratching. The same article says a long or double coat may need brushing and a blow dryer where a short coat gets by with a towel, and it advises removing mats because they hold moisture next to the skin.

    The five places that hide water

    • Behind and under the ears: soft, feathery hair under a flap that shuts out air.
    • The ruff and chest: the deepest hair at the front, and often the first part into the water.
    • Armpits and groin: folds where skin touches skin.
    • The pants and under the tail: long feathering that gets sat on while still wet.
    • Feet: hair between pads collects water, sand and grit.

    How to test it with your hand

    Part the hair with two fingers or a comb until you see skin. Touch the skin with the back of a finger. If it feels cool and tacky, or the hair near the root clumps together, that zone is not done. Check several spots in each area, because one dry parting says little about the next.

    What is the best step-by-step way to dry a Berner?

    Work in four phases: remove loose water, rest on absorbent bedding, move air to the skin, then comb and re-check. Each phase makes the next one shorter and easier on your dog.

    Phase 1: remove the loose water

    Let your dog shake first, ideally outdoors or in the bathroom with the door shut. Then press and squeeze a towel against the coat instead of rubbing in circles, which can tangle long hair. Squeeze down each leg, wrap the chest, and lift the tail to blot the pants. Swap to a fresh towel when the first stops picking up water. Larger, thick-coated dogs benefit from absorbent towels, and having a stack ready beats hunting for one mid-job.

    Phase 2: a short recharge break

    Give your dog a few quiet minutes on a dry towel or washable bed in a warm, draft-free room. Offer water and a chew. You get a breather, and your dog arrives at the dryer settled instead of wound up. Do not treat this as the finish: air alone reaches the deep layer slowly.

    Phase 3: move air down to the skin

    Start at the rear end and work forward, so your dog meets the noise at the far end from their face. With a high velocity dryer, hold the nozzle roughly perpendicular to the coat, move it slowly and let each parting open up before you move on. Groomer Jennifer Bishop-Jenkins, writing in Groomer to Groomer, warns that whipping the nozzle back and forth creates little knots and actually lengthens drying time.

    With a human-style hand dryer, use the cool or warm setting, never hot, keep it moving gently, and part the hair with your other hand so the air gets in. Your hand in the parting is also your heat gauge: if it is uncomfortable for you, it is too hot for the skin.

    Phase 4: comb and re-check

    Once the coat is mostly dry, line comb: push a section of hair up, then comb it down a thin layer at a time from the skin outward. This opens the undercoat, shows you any remaining damp, and lifts loose hair before it mats. Finish with the skin check in the tracker above, then look again later in the day at the ears, armpits and pants.

    Should you use a towel, a hair dryer or a force dryer?

    Towels are for removing water, a hand dryer can finish small areas, and a pet force dryer is the practical choice if your Berner swims or gets bathed often. The right one is also the one your dog will tolerate.

    Noise and who should skip the force dryer

    These dryers have no heating element, although the motor gently warms the air. Their main drawback is noise. The Groomer to Groomer article says anyone nearby needs hearing protection, suggests a hooded wrap to shield the dog's head and ears, and says to switch the high velocity dryer off for the head and use a gentler dryer there. It also says high velocity dryers should not be used on young puppies, seniors or dogs with stressful health conditions.

    That caution matters when you watch the video above, which shows introducing a puppy to the sound. Ask your own veterinarian or groomer before you point any strong airflow at a young, old or unwell dog. For gentle noise practice, let your dog hear the machine from another room, reward calm, and stop while it is still easy.

    How does the routine change after rain, swimming or a bath?

    Rain mostly soaks the outer coat and the underside, natural water and pools need a rinse first, and a bath leaves the whole coat wet to the skin. Match the effort to how deep the water went.

    After a rainy walk

    Keep a "door station": two towels on a hook, a mat that catches drips and a washable bed. A quick blot every time is kinder than one big session after the coat has stayed damp all day. If the water has clearly reached the skin, treat it like a swim. For planning outings around wet weather, our notes on real Berner walking routines and hiking distances owners manage help you decide how long to stay out.

    After a swim

    Rinse before you drive home if you can, and line the car with towels. A wet Berner in a closed crate keeps lying on the same damp hair, so dry the belly and pants before loading, then continue at home. If loading a tired dog is its own battle, see our guide to calmer car rides and loading.

    Watch the tail after a cold swim too. Limber tail, also called swimmer's tail, is a painful, floppy tail that usually shows up within about a day of swimming in water that is too cold. It is seen mostly in sporting and working dogs. A limp or painful tail is a reason to call your vet, not something to massage out.

    After a bath

    Brush before the water goes on, since mats get harder to open once wet. Rinse longer than feels necessary, towel, then dry fully. If a professional handles baths, ask how they confirm the undercoat is dry; our list of what to ask a Berner groomer covers the rest of the visit.

    What about the ears and feet?

    Dry them by hand and gently: blot the inside of the ear flap with a soft towel or cotton ball, and press water out from between the toes. Do not push anything into the ear canal or aim strong air at it.

    An emergency veterinarian quoted in this Adopt a Pet article on drying after baths cautions that a hair dryer aimed around the ears can force more water in. Another vet in the same piece suggests gently drying the ears with cotton balls for dogs prone to ear infections. Cornell adds that wet ears from swimming can lead to infections and then to hot spots under the ear, and advises a daily look for redness, moisture or discharge.

    Feet routine

    Lift one paw at a time, wrap it in a towel and squeeze. Spread the toes and look for seeds, stones or redness. Finish with brief cool air if your dog accepts it, and comb the hair between the pads so it does not felt together.

    Does a Berner need to be completely dry?

    For a thick double coat, aim for dry at the skin, especially in the folds. A slightly cool outer tip of hair is less of a concern than a damp patch you cannot see.

    Advice differs here, and it helps to know why. One veterinarian in the Adopt a Pet piece says dogs do not need to be completely dry after a bath and can finish in a warm area, while adding that dogs with a history of hot spots, allergies or skin conditions could benefit from extra drying. Cornell's warning is aimed at thick coats that do not dry fully. A Bernese is a thick-coated dog, so the cautious choice is to finish the job, particularly in hot, humid weather.

    When to stop and call the vet

    • A red, moist, sore patch your dog keeps licking or scratching. Cornell notes that a sore more than 24 hours old is likely infected and needs a veterinary visit.
    • Head shaking, ear scratching, odor or discharge from an ear.
    • A tail held limp or obvious pain around its base.
    • Shivering, unusual tiredness or anything that worries you after a cold swim.

    Do not blow-dry over irritated skin or put home remedies on it while you wait. Keep your dog from chewing the area and let the clinic advise you.

    Your repeatable drying checklist

    Keeping the rhythm sustainable

    Split the work if either of you is fading: finish the high-risk folds first, take a break, then do the broad back and sides. Pair each pause with something your dog likes. Over a few weeks, the sound of the dryer and the sight of the towel stack can become a signal for calm. Dry weather days are a good time for practice runs of one minute, with no water at all. Regular brushing also makes every later drying session easier; our home routine for Berner shedding and the coat calendar help you keep it on schedule.

    Common drying mistakes

    • Trusting the topcoat. Always part to the skin before you call it done.
    • Vigorous rubbing. It can twist long hair into tangles that then hold water.
    • Using hot air to hurry. Stay on cool or warm and keep the air moving.
    • Drying over mats. Water stays trapped underneath. Work them out gently or ask a groomer.
    • Wiggling a force dryer nozzle. Slow, steady passes do more.
    • Letting the dog sleep on a soaked bed. Swap it, or the belly stays damp.
    • Shaving to save time. Clipping a double coat is not a drying shortcut; talk to a groomer about coat care instead.
    • Forcing a frightened dog. Stop, use towels and a warm room, and rebuild comfort in small steps.

    Bernese drying FAQ

    How long does it take to dry a Bernese Mountain Dog?

    There is no reliable single figure. It depends on how deep the water went, coat density, mats, your tool and your dog's patience. Go by the skin check, not the clock.

    Can I let my Berner air dry after rain?

    If only the outer hair is damp, blotting and a warm room may be enough. If the skin or undercoat is wet, move air through the coat, because thick coats that stay damp are linked with hot spots.

    Can I use my own hair dryer on my dog?

    On a cool or warm setting, kept moving, and away from the ear canals. Stop if your dog is distressed or the skin feels hot.

    Is a force dryer safe for a Bernese puppy?

    The groomer author cited above advises against high velocity dryers for young puppies, seniors and dogs with stressful health conditions. Use towels and ask your veterinarian or groomer.

    Why does my Berner still smell wet the next day?

    Damp may be left in the undercoat, bedding or ears. Part the coat and check. A strong or unusual odor, redness or scratching calls for a veterinary check.

    Should I bathe my dog after every swim?

    Not necessarily. A thorough clean-water rinse is the usual first step after a swim. Ask your veterinarian or groomer how often shampoo suits your dog's skin.

    A calmer end to every wet day

    To dry a Bernese Mountain Dog without losing the day to a wet dog, keep the order simple: rinse if needed, press, pause, move air to the skin, comb and re-check. Set up your door station this week, try one quiet dryer practice with no water, and let the routine do the heavy lifting the next time your Berner finds a puddle.
