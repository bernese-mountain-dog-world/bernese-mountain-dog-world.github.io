Will a Bernese Mountain Dog Crate Fit in Your SUV? A Dog, Crate and Cargo-Space Checklist

Four tape-measure numbers on the dog, four on the crate and five on the car. Here is how to check them before you buy a crate, or a new SUV, that will not close.

Home›Blog›Bernese Mountain Dog Crate in an SUV

Published: 2026-10-06 · Bernese Mountain Dog World

If you have ever stood behind your open hatch with a tape measure, wondering whether a Bernese Mountain Dog crate will fit in your SUV, you are in good company. It is a bit like buying a sofa for a third-floor walk-up: the sofa fits the room fine, but the stairwell decides. With a 100-pound Berner, the stairwell is your cargo opening, your wheel wells and the slope of your rear glass.

Quick answer: It depends on three separate fits, not one. The crate's interior has to fit your dog, the crate's exterior (with handles and door hinges) has to pass through the lowest point of the hatch opening and sit between the wheel wells, and the crate plus your dog has to be tied down to anchors that are actually rated for that load. Most "it doesn't fit" stories fail on height at the opening, not on floor length.

This guide gives you a measuring worksheet, a calculator that runs the numbers for you, real crate dimensions from manufacturers, and the fit-or-no-fit patterns Bernese owners keep reporting. We do not list "SUVs that fit a Berner crate," because cargo shapes change between model years and trims, and a list like that would be guesswork. Your tape measure is more honest than any list.

Why does a Berner crate fail to fit so often?

Because the crate many Berners sleep in at home was never designed for a car. A common story in the Bernese owner community goes like this one, from an r/bernesemountaindogs thread about mid-sized SUVs: a 48-inch-long, 32-inch-high metal crate did not fit in the back of a 2018 Nissan Pathfinder, even though the owner's male Berner is on the small side at about 25 inches at the withers and 105 pounds, and his head already grazes the crate's top. The owner described the cargo area as "very rounded," with second-row seats that do not fold flat.

You might feel like the car is the problem. Often it is the mismatch between two goals. A home crate is sized so your dog can stand and turn around. A travel kennel is often sized tighter on purpose. Gunner, for example, tells buyers its travel kennel should be a "snug fit" with the dog "curled up in your kennel, NOT spread out" (Gunner size chart). Same dog, two different right answers.

How big is an adult Bernese Mountain Dog, really?

Start with the breed range, then replace it with your own dog's numbers. According to the AKC breed standard, males stand 25 to 27.5 inches at the shoulder and females 23 to 26 inches, with males typically 80 to 115 pounds and females 70 to 95 pounds.

Measuring your dog in two minutes

  1. Length: with your dog standing square, measure from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail (not the tip).
  2. Withers height: floor to the top of the shoulder blades, the highest point of the back.
  3. Sitting head height: floor to the top of the head while your dog sits. This is the number that tells you if a low-roofed cargo area will force a lie-down ride.
  4. Weight: use your vet's scale reading, not a guess.

Two manufacturer rules of thumb help here. MeasuredPet suggests adding a 2 to 4 inch margin to nose-to-tail length and checking it against the crate's interior, not exterior, size. The MIM Safe Variocage sizing guide adds 3 inches to both length and withers height (Variocage car sizing guide, PDF). For a travel kennel, follow the specific maker's guidance, because some intend a tighter fit.

How do you measure your SUV's cargo area for a crate?

Measure the car as if the crate were a sofa going through a doorway. The Variocage car sizing guide spells out a method that works for any hard crate:

  1. Opening height: from the cargo floor straight up to the lowest point of the hatch opening, measured at the center of the door frame, not at an angle. This is usually the tightest number.
  2. Narrowest floor width: often between the rear wheel wells or at the bottom of the hatch entrance, wherever the crate will actually sit.
  3. Usable floor length: from the back of the rear seat to the door sill, with the seats in the position you will really drive with.
  4. Length at crate-top height: repeat the length measurement at the height of the crate's roof. Reclined seatbacks and sloped rear glass steal length up high, which is why a crate can sit flat on the floor and still block the hatch from closing.
  5. Sill height: note whether the sill is raised above the cargo floor. The Variocage guide suggests a flat spacer, such as rigid foam board, under a crate so its doors clear a raised sill.

Write the numbers on paper with the date and seat position. Cargo specs published as "cubic feet" are no help here. A tall, boxy 30 cubic feet and a sloped, wide 30 cubic feet behave completely differently with a 33-inch-tall box.

    Can you keep the back seats up for the kids?

    This is the question behind most owner threads, and it is where you might feel stuck between a minivan and a dog-free family trip. One owner in a Bernese car-safety discussion said they would love a crash-tested cage but did not want to "permanently lose 80-90%" of their cargo space. That is a real trade-off, and it helps to see it as three scenarios.

    Seat setupWhat has to be trueTypical trade-off
    All rows up, crate behind the last rowFloor length behind the last row, at crate-top height, is longer than the crate's exterior lengthKeeps every seat, but luggage has to go on the roof or in a hitch box
    Third row folded (three-row SUVs)Folded section is flat and the crate does not ride on the seatback hingeSecond row stays for kids; the crate takes the space the third row used
    One side of a split second row foldedFolded section is wider than the crate's exterior width plus room for strapsFrees one seat and part of the cargo floor; check that the crate is not resting on a sloped seatback

    Owners report all three working in different cars, which is exactly why a model list is unreliable. In the first thread, one owner hauls their Berner in a Ruff Land XL in a 2019 Subaru Outback; another fits a crate in a VW Tiguan only with the third row down. Measure your own folded configuration, because "folds flat" in a brochure can still leave a 2-inch step.

    What is left for luggage?

    Subtract the crate's exterior width from your narrowest floor width. If the remainder is under the width of your usual suitcase or stroller, that gear rides elsewhere. Also hold the crate's exterior height against your rear window: if the crate is taller than the bottom of the glass, it will block your rearview mirror, so plan on side mirrors or a camera mirror.

    How will a 100-pound Berner get into a crate that high?

    The crate floor sits on top of the cargo floor, which already sits well above the ground in most SUVs. Lifting a Berner in and out every trip is hard on your back and on a giant breed's elbows and hips, which are a known weak point for the breed (see our Bernese Mountain Dog care guide). Owners in these threads mention fold-out stairs or step units to avoid daily jumping, and one owner called the height of a large luxury SUV its "fatal flaw" because getting their Berner in and out was so hard.

    Before you buy, measure from the ground to the top of the cargo sill, then add the crate's floor thickness. That combined number is the jump your dog will make. If your dog is a senior, recovering from surgery or simply hesitant, practice with a ramp at home before the first long drive.

    Are your SUV's cargo tie-downs strong enough?

    This is the step most fit guides skip. The Center for Pet Safety warns that "cargo area anchors are not always weight-rated for the forces generated in an accident," and recommends asking your vehicle manufacturer whether your anchors have a strength rating before relying on them (CPS cargo area connection advisory). CPS also reminds owners that crash force is mass times acceleration, and that the mass is the dog plus the crate.

    Run that with real numbers. A 105-pound male in a 72-pound Gunner G1 Large is 177 pounds sitting still, and a crash multiplies that load many times over. A 100-pound dog in a 42-pound Ruff Land XL is 142 pounds. Either way, the small grocery-bag hooks in many cargo areas were never designed for that job.

    • Ask the carmaker for the rated load of each cargo anchor, in writing if possible.
    • Use strength-rated anchor straps from the crate maker, not luggage bungees. CPS testing found proper anchor straps significantly reduced crate movement into the seatback.
    • Skip extension tethers and ziplines on a harnessed dog in the cargo area; CPS says they increase the risk of injury to you, your passengers and your pet.

    Impact tested is not the same as certified

    Many crates are marketed as "impact tested" or "crash tested," which describes the maker's own testing. Independent certification is a narrower list. As of October 2026, the CPS certified products list for travel crates includes the Gunner G1 Small, Medium and Intermediate, the Cabela's GunDog Kennel Intermediate and Large, and the Lucky Duck Lucky Kennel Medium, Intermediate and Large. The Gunner G1 Large, Ruff Land and Impact crates in our table do not appear on that list. That does not make them unsafe; it means you should read exactly what each maker tested and under which conditions, rather than treat the labels as equal.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What size crate does a Bernese Mountain Dog need for the car?

    Measure your dog's nose-to-tail-base length and withers height, then compare them to the crate's interior. MeasuredPet suggests a 48-inch crate as a common starting size for home use, but travel kennels are often shorter and snugger by design, so follow the specific maker's sizing guide.

    Will a 48-inch dog crate fit in a mid-size SUV?

    Sometimes, but it is the size most likely to fail. A 48-inch-long, 32-inch-high metal crate did not fit one owner's 2018 Nissan Pathfinder because of a rounded cargo area and seats that do not fold flat. Measure opening height and floor length at crate-top height first.

    Which measurement usually stops a crate from fitting?

    The hatch opening height at its lowest point. Crates over 33 inches tall with handles often hit the top of the opening, even when the floor is long enough.

    Can my Bernese ride in the crate with the back seats up?

    Only if the floor behind the last seat row, measured at crate-top height, is longer than the crate's exterior length. If not, folding one side of a split seat or the third row is the usual compromise.

    Are SUV cargo hooks strong enough to strap down a dog crate?

    Not necessarily. The Center for Pet Safety warns that cargo anchors are not always weight-rated for crash forces and advises asking your vehicle manufacturer about anchor strength, then using strength-rated anchor straps.

    Is an impact-tested crate the same as a certified crate?

    No. Impact-tested usually describes a manufacturer's own testing. Independent certification, such as the Center for Pet Safety program, covers a smaller list of specific crate sizes, so check the exact model and size.

    How do I get a heavy Bernese into a high SUV crate?

    Measure the ground-to-sill height plus the crate floor, and if that jump is high for your dog, use a ramp or step unit and practice at home first. Repeated jumping is hard on a giant breed's joints.

    Crate specifications change, so confirm current figures on the maker's own page before you order.
