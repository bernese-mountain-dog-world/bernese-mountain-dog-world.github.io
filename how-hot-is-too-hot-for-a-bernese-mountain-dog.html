How Hot Is Too Hot for a Bernese Mountain Dog?

How hot is too hot for a Bernese Mountain Dog? Learn when to shorten the walk, when to skip it, and which heat-warning signs owners should not ignore.

Home›Blog›How Hot Is Too Hot for a Bernese Mountain Dog?

Published: 2026-05-21 · Bernese Mountain Dog World

Bernese Mountain Dogs, with their thick double coats and heavy frames, can quickly overheat in warm weather, so understanding how hot is too hot for a Bernese Mountain Dog is essential for keeping them safe from May through September. Unlike short-haired breeds, a Bernese has a dense, insulating undercoat and a long, coarse outer coat that were designed for the Swiss Alps, not a humid summer afternoon. Because their cooling system relies heavily on panting and limited sweating through their paw pads, they struggle to regulate body temperature when the air is warm, still, or humid. This guide will give you clear temperature bands, practical walk-skip rules, pavement checks, and the five heat-stress warning signs that tell you to stop everything and act fast.

Quick answer: For a Bernese Mountain Dog, any temperature above 75°F (24°C) carries a real risk of overheating, especially when humidity pushes the combined heat index past 150. At 80°F (27°C) or higher, skip the walk entirely, even if your dog seems eager. Use the pavement five-second test before every warm-weather outing.

Why Bernese Mountain Dogs Overheat Faster Than Other Breeds

The genetics and body structure of the Bernese Mountain Dog make them particularly vulnerable to heat. Their thick double coat acts as insulation for both cold and heat, which means warm air gets trapped against their skin. Unlike a Labrador or a Greyhound, a Bernese cannot cool off quickly through their skin because the coat traps heat against the body. Additionally, their large chest and heavy bone structure generate more metabolic heat during exercise. A Bernese that is overweight or has a thicker coat than expected will feel the heat even sooner. Owner studies and breed information from the American Kennel Club confirm this breed is best suited to cool climates, and many individual Bernese show visible discomfort by 70°F when the sun is direct.

Three Temperature Bands Every Owner Needs

Instead of guessing, use these three decision bands for any day your Bernese wants to go outside. Each band assumes your dog is a healthy adult (one to seven years old) with no known breathing or heart issues. If your dog is a puppy under one year, a senior over seven, or has a history of panting problems, shift your decision to the next warmer band.

Temperature Band Activity Allowed Precautions Needed
Below 65°F (18°C) Normal walks, hikes, playtime Bring water for longer outings
65°F – 75°F (18°C – 24°C) Short walks, avoid midday sun Walk on grass or shaded paths, check pavement
Above 75°F (24°C) Only potty breaks outside Skip all exercise; use fans or air conditioning

This second table adds humidity and direct sun into the equation, which is critical because humidity stops panting from working properly.

Temperature Humidity Verdict for a Bernese
70°F Below 50% Brief walk possible in shade
70°F Above 60% Skip the walk; high heat stress
75°F Any humidity Do not walk
80°F Any humidity Emergency risk; stay inside

Pavement Heat: The Five-Second Test

A Bernese Mountain Dog's paw pads are tough, but they are not heatproof. Asphalt and concrete can reach 125°F on a sunny 75°F day, and direct contact for more than a minute can cause burns. The simple way to check is the five-second test: press the back of your hand flat on the pavement for five full seconds. If you cannot keep your hand there, the surface is too hot for your dog. Even if the air feels comfortable, pavement can burn paws before you notice your dog limping. Bernese tend to push through discomfort because they want to stay with you, so do not rely on their reaction. Always walk on grass or dirt during warm months, and carry a portable water dish for paw cooling if you cannot avoid pavement crossings. Best Times for Summer Walks

When you live with a Bernese Mountain Dog, your walk schedule should shift with the sun. The two windows that work are early morning (before 7 a.m.) and late evening (after 8 p.m.), when the sun is low and the ground has cooled. During those times, temperatures are often 10 to 15 degrees cooler than midday, and humidity tends to be lower. If you wake up and the temperature is already above 70°F, skip the morning walk and have indoor play instead. For evening walks, wait until the pavement feels cool underfoot rather than going by the clock. Bringing frozen water bottles, a cooling vest, or a damp towel can add margin of safety, but nothing replaces avoiding heat exposure entirely. If your dog pulls toward a puddle or a sprinkler, let them cool off briefly before heading home.

Five Overheating Signs You Should Not Ignore

Recognizing the first signs of overheating is the most practical skill a Bernese owner can learn. These dogs are stoic and may not show distress until they are dangerously close to collapse. Watch for these five signs in order of urgency:

  • Excessive panting that does not stop: Panting is normal, but when it does not slow down after a drink or a rest, that is a warning.
  • Thick, ropey drool: Normal Bernese drool is thin and watery; heat stress thickens it into strings.
  • Bright red or very pale gums: Lift your dog's lip and check; normal gums are pink like your own.
  • Stumbling or wobbling: A loss of coordination means the brain is affected, and this is a medical emergency.
  • Vomiting or diarrhea: Heatstroke often triggers vomiting or loose stool as the body crashes.

If you see any of these signs, stop the activity immediately. Move your dog to shade or an air-conditioned space. Offer cool (not ice-cold) water to drink. Wet a towel with tepid water and place it on their belly, armpits, and paw pads. Do not use ice water, which can cause blood vessels to constrict and trap heat inside. Then call your veterinarian right away. Heatstroke can cause organ damage within minutes, and your dog may need IV fluids and monitoring. For general safety advice about overheating across all breeds, the AKC has a thorough guide on overheating that applies to Bernese as well.

Heat and Your Bernese’s Health History

Heat safety is not a one-size-fits-all rule. A Bernese with a known heart murmur, a history of laryngeal paralysis, or a respiratory issue like brachycephalic airway syndrome (though less common in Bernese) will have an even lower heat threshold. If your dog is on any medication, especially diuretics, pain relievers, or steroids, ask your vet whether those drugs affect heat tolerance. The same applies to dogs recovering from a recent illness or surgery. Cooling Gear That Actually Works

You do not need expensive gadgets to help your Bernese handle warm weather, but a few proven tools can make summer safer. A cooling mat that activates with pressure (no electricity needed) gives your dog a comfortable place to lie down after a walk. A wide-brimmed dog hat or a UV-protective shirt can help for dogs that must be outside with you, but never use them as an excuse to keep your dog in the heat longer. A portable water bottle with a built-in bowl ensures you always have a drink available. Avoid soaking your Bernese completely before a walk though – wet coats in hot, humid air can trap heat rather than release it. The most effective cooling method is still shade, airflow, and access to cold water indoors. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I walk my Bernese in 80°F weather if we stick to the shade?

No. At 80°F, shade provides minimal relief because the air temperature alone is high enough to cause overheating. The cooling effect of panting diminishes significantly above 80°F, and shade does not lower the air temperature. Skip the walk entirely.

How do I know if my Bernese is overheating or just tired?

Look at their panting rhythm. A tired dog pants for a minute or two and then lies down and breathes normally. An overheating dog pants rapidly and will not stop even when lying still. Check their gum color and drool thickness. If they are drooling thick ropes, that is heat stress, not just normal end-of-walk tiredness.

Is it safe for my Bernese to swim on a hot day?

Swimming can be safer than walking because water cools the body, but exercise in the water still raises core temperature. If the air is above 80°F, the water alone may not prevent overheating, and your dog could still get heatstroke from vigorous swimming. Limit swim time to 10 minutes and watch for heavy panting.

Should I shave my Bernese Mountain Dog for summer?

No. Shaving a Bernese Mountain Dog ruins their natural insulation and exposes their skin to sunburn and insect bites. Their double coat keeps them cooler in summer by trapping a layer of cool air near the skin. Regular brushing to remove loose undercoat is the correct approach.

What indoor temperature is safe for a Bernese during summer?

Keep your home below 75°F if possible. If you do not have air conditioning, use fans at floor level (Bernese lie low) and provide access to cool tile floors. Never leave your Bernese in a car, even for five minutes, even if the windows are cracked and the temperature outside is below 65°F.

Does humidity really matter that much for a Bernese?

Yes, humidity matters enormously. Panting relies on moisture evaporation from the tongue and respiratory tract. When humidity is above 60%, evaporation slows down dramatically, which means your dog cannot cool off even if they are panting hard. Check a combined heat index before deciding to walk.

What do I do if my Bernese collapses from heat?

Call your vet immediately or go to an emergency animal hospital. While traveling, wet their belly and paw pads with cool (not icy) water and get them into an air-conditioned car. Do not force them to drink if they are unconscious. Pouring ice water over them can cause shock, so stick to cool water only.

How can I check the heat before heading out?

Use any weather app that shows the heat index or apparent temperature. Pay more attention to the heat index than the plain air temperature. If the heat index is above 80°F, stay inside. Also check pavement temperature with your hand as described earlier.
