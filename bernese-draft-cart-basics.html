Draft Cart Basics for Bernese Mountain Dogs: Gear, Harness Fit, First Sessions and How to Find a Draft Club

Home›Guides›Bernese draft cart basics

Learning draft cart basics for Bernese Mountain Dogs means teaching three things in order: a few reliable cues, comfort with gear that follows the dog around, and only then a real cart. The Bernese Mountain Dog Club of America (BMDCA) puts numbers on the finish line: a dog must be at least 2 years old to enter its draft tests, and a first-level team hauls just 20 pounds over half a mile.

Quick answer: Start with cues without any equipment, let your dog wear the harness, add light dragging traces, then shafts, then an empty cart with a helper. Keep each session short and end on something your dog does well. For titles, the BMDCA minimum age is 2 years, the Novice load is 20 lb, and the Open load is the dog's own weight rounded down to the nearest 10 lb, with a 60 lb minimum.

    Draft work is a team job: the handler walks beside, behind or in front of the dog while the dog pulls an empty or loaded rig. This guide covers the gear, how a harness should fit, a progression for first sessions, what a test actually asks, and where to find people who will let you watch before you buy anything.

    Before you try it at home, watch how slowly a trainer goes. In this short lesson from SitStay with Stacy Slade, a young dog named Deuce is still nervous about the cart and shafts, and a confident dog helps show what it is all about.

    What gear do you need for Bernese draft cart basics?

    You need a draft harness that leaves the shoulders free, a rig (usually a two-wheeled cart or four-wheeled wagon), an ordinary collar and leash, and rewards. A beginner does not need to buy the rig first.

    The magazine Whole Dog Journal describes carting as a dog pulling a two-wheeled cart or a four-wheeled wagon while a person walks alongside. Its overview of carting and draft training put a well-made harness at $60 to $300 or more, a cart or wagon from about $350, and home-built carts as low as $100. Those figures come from an article first published in 2009 and updated in 2019, so treat them as a floor and get current quotes. The same piece notes that many people lend newcomers a cart until they have their own.

    Three harness styles and how they differ

    Harness styleWhat it looks likeWhere the pull connectsQuestion to ask when trying it on
    ParadePadded strap across the shoulders that also encircles the chest, plus a second padded strap across the frontAsk the maker where traces and shaft loops attachDoes the front strap stay clear of the throat and the moving front legs?
    Draft (collar type)Padded collar around the neck and shoulders, with a belly strapTraces run from the collar to the rig; the belly strap connects to the shaftDoes the collar sit flat without rocking when the dog walks?
    SiwashSimilar to a sled-dog harness, with straps along the back and a padded chest strap along the breastbone and between the front legsAsk the maker where the traces clip onIs the back long enough, or do straps crowd the hips and tail?

    The one rule that matters across all three: choose a harness that is comfortable and does not restrict the free motion of the shoulders and legs. An everyday walking harness is not designed for hauling, so do not hitch a cart to one.

    Cart or wagon?

    A cart has two wheels, so how you place the load changes how much rests on the shafts. A wagon has four wheels and carries the load itself, but it is longer to turn and back. The BMDCA rules allow a cart, wagon, sled, toboggan or travois, and every one of them must be set up so it cannot run up on the dog going downhill. Ask any seller how their rig meets that requirement.

    How should a draft harness fit a Bernese Mountain Dog?

    It should sit snugly without rubbing, keep pressure on padded areas of the chest and shoulders, and let the front legs swing at full stride. Check it while your dog walks and turns, not only while they stand.

    A Berner is broad, deep-chested and heavily coated, so a harness that looks right on the table can sit very differently on the dog. Use this fitting routine with the maker's instructions beside you:

    1. Brush first. Mats and thick undercoat change how straps lie. Part the coat when you check strap edges.
    2. Front. The breast or collar piece should not press into the throat when the dog leans forward.
    3. Elbows and armpits. Run your fingers behind the front legs. Straps that touch the elbow on each stride will rub.
    4. Stride check. Have someone walk the dog away and back while you watch for a shortened step or a twisted strap.
    5. Shafts. Once hitched, shafts should run alongside the body without poking the shoulders. Shaft height and spread may need adjusting between dogs.
    6. Traces. Both sides should be the same length so the rig follows straight.
    7. After the session. Look and feel for rubbed coat or tender spots. If you find any, change the fit before the next session.

    If you cannot tell whether the fit is right, that is the best reason to attend a club workshop. An experienced eye will spot a collar that rocks or a trace set too high in seconds.

    How do you run the first draft sessions?

    Build one layer at a time: cues, harness, traces, shafts, then cart. Move on only when your dog is relaxed at the current step, and drop back a step the moment they look worried.

    Step 1: cues before equipment

    Teach forward, faster, slower, turn right, turn left, back up and stop before the cart exists in your dog's world. Practice on leash, then off leash in a secure area, and then with something between you, such as a low wall or a line of buckets. The barrier rehearses the odd distance you will have when a rig sits between you and the dog.

    Step 2: harness as ordinary clothing

    Let your dog sniff the harness, reward calm, and put it on for a moment. Extend gradually to wearing it around the house and on walks. Some dogs accept this in one evening; others need many short repeats.

    Step 3: traces, then light drag

    Clip two leashes to the harness as stand-in traces. Walk beside your dog while a friend holds the ends behind, a bit like carrying a train. Your friend adds slight pressure while you reward forward movement. Later, tie something light to each trace, such as a plastic jug with a little sand. The friend follows closely enough to pick it up if your dog startles.

    Step 4: shafts

    Long poles beside the body feel different from anything pulling behind. Introduce practice shafts gently, dragging them first and moving them forward over several sessions. Then let your dog walk between the real shafts while you pull the cart yourself. Teach turns here too, using wide arcs.

    Step 5: first hitch, empty cart

    Choose flat, quiet, fenced ground. Ask a helper to walk beside or behind the cart, ready to steady it. Keep the dog on leash, take a few steps, reward and stop. Unhitch while it is still going well. Weight comes much later, in small increases, and it is worth asking your veterinarian before any loaded work.

    What does a BMDCA draft test ask a team to do?

    A team must pass every exercise in its class at a single test: control and harnessing, a maneuvering course, slow pace, back-up, distractions, loading, a group stay and a half-mile freight haul. The details below come from the BMDCA Draft Test Regulations, January 2023 edition; confirm the current edition before you enter.

    Class and titleWho may enterLeashFreight haul load (half mile)
    Novice Draft (NDD)Dogs of eligible age, with or without earlier titlesOn leash, except Harness and Hitch and Group Stay20 lb
    Open Draft (DD)Dogs holding NDDAll exercises off leashOwn weight rounded down to nearest 10 lb, or 60 lb, whichever is greater
    Brace Novice (BNDD)Two dogs that each hold NDDAs Novice, without Basic Control40 lb per brace
    Brace Open (BDD)Two dogs that each hold DD and BNDDAs OpenSum of both dogs' Open loads

    Exercises and the numbers inside them

    • Basic Control (Novice Draft only): walking within arm's length with changes of direction and speed, plus an off-leash recall of about 30 feet.
    • Harness and Hitch: the dog cooperates while being harnessed and hitched. In Open, the dog also waits while the handler brings in the rig.
    • Maneuvering: circles and 90-degree turns both ways, a movable obstacle such as a gate or branch, and "narrows" only 12 inches wider than the team's widest measurement, with no contact.
    • Slow: a visible change of the dog's pace for at least 10 feet.
    • Back-up: at least 1 foot for Novice and 4 feet for Open, within one minute.
    • Distractions: one mainly auditory and one mainly visual, about 10 feet away.
    • Group stay: 3 minutes hitched to a loaded rig; Novice handlers stand across the ring, Open handlers go out of sight.
    • Freight haul: a half-mile course over natural terrain, ideally with hills and changes of surface. A judge can stop it if the load is too much for the dog in the conditions.

    Rules that surprise first-timers

    • Carrying food or toys during an exercise fails the run, so fade pocket rewards well before test day.
    • Collars must be buckle, slip or martingale style. Prong and electronic collars are not allowed.
    • No person or animal may serve as freight. Load weight must be verifiable on a scale and secured in the rig.
    • Open entries need a weight certificate signed and dated within 60 days before each test date.
    • Bitches in season may not compete.
    • Draft tests are open to all purebred or mixed dogs registered or listed with the American or Canadian Kennel Club; National Specialty draft tests are Berners only.

    Beyond the four basic titles, passing a class five times under at least seven different judges earns an Advanced or Master title, and the two Advanced plus two Master titles together make a Grand Master Draft Dog.

    How do you find a Bernese draft club?

    Start with the BMDCA event list and your regional Bernese club, then go and watch without your dog. Clubs also take questions about workshops that are not yet posted.

    The BMDCA nearby events page can be filtered by "Draft Test" and by region. When checked, its draft tests for the first half of 2027 included clubs in Arizona (Grand Canyon State), Southern California, Northern California (Sierra West), North Carolina (Blue Ridge), Colorado (Rockies), Pennsylvania (Mason Dixon) and Iowa (Heartland), plus club-run tests in Missouri. Most regional entries were open to all breeds with Berners given preference; the Missouri listings were Berner only. Dates and closing times change, so rely on the premium list for the event itself.

    What to do on your first visit

    • Arrive without your dog and watch a harnessing, a back-up and a freight haul.
    • Ask which harness styles members use on dogs built like yours, and why.
    • Ask whether anyone lends a rig or measures harnesses at workshops.
    • Ask when and where the group practices, and whether beginners can join.
    • Check the club's rules for entry age and equipment. Other organizations use different titles and rules from the BMDCA.

    Getting there is part of the plan, especially with a cart in the back. If your dog is unsettled on the road, work through our guide to calm Bernese car rides, and measure your crate setup against the SUV crate fit checklist before deciding where the rig will ride.

    Common draft training mistakes

    • Buying the cart first. Watch a practice, borrow if you can, and learn which width and shaft style suit your dog.
    • Skipping the back-up. It is much easier to teach without a rig, then add it with plenty of help.
    • Hitching on day one. A cart that rattles and follows can frighten a dog who has never felt anything behind them.
    • No helper. A startled dog can tip a rig or run with it. A second person can catch the cart.
    • Tight turns too early. A pole bumping the shoulder teaches the dog that turning is unpleasant.
    • Adding weight to show progress. Calm starts, halts and turns are the progress. Load is the last variable.
    • Training past the dog's interest. If your dog does not enjoy it, change the plan or stop. The test rules themselves look for willingness.
    • Letting children ride. BMDCA freight rules bar people and animals as loads; do not treat a training cart as a ride.

    Draft cart FAQ for Bernese owners

    How old must a Bernese Mountain Dog be for a draft test?

    At least 2 years old on the day of a BMDCA draft test. Other organizations set their own rules.

    How much weight does a dog pull in a Novice Draft test?

    A Novice Draft dog pulls 20 pounds on a half-mile freight haul. A Novice brace pulls 40 pounds.

    How heavy is the Open Draft load for a 95-pound Berner?

    90 pounds: the dog's weight rounded down to the nearest ten. Dogs under 70 pounds pull the 60-pound minimum.

    Do I need a special harness for carting?

    Yes. Use a parade, draft or siwash style made for hauling, fitted so shoulders and legs move freely, not an everyday walking harness.

    Can a mixed-breed dog enter a BMDCA draft test?

    Yes, if registered or listed with the American or Canadian Kennel Club. National Specialty draft tests are limited to Bernese Mountain Dogs, and some events give Berners preference.

    Can I start training before my dog is 2?

    The 2-year rule applies to test entry. Foundation skills such as cues and comfort around gear can be taught earlier; ask your breeder, club mentor and veterinarian before your dog pulls anything with weight.

    Can I use food rewards in the test?

    No. Carrying food or toys during an exercise results in a failing performance. Verbal praise is allowed, and gentle physical praise between exercises.

    Your next step with a draft cart

    Draft cart basics for Bernese Mountain Dogs come down to patience in the right order: cues, harness, traces, shafts, then an empty cart with a helper. This week, pick one cue your dog does not yet own and practice it for a few minutes. This month, find a draft test or workshop and watch. The gear decisions get much easier once you have seen real teams move.
